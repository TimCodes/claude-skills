# Investment Vehicles and Account Types

Two independent axes that beginners conflate: **what** you invest in (the vehicle) and **where**
you hold it (the account, which determines the tax treatment). The same fund behaves very
differently in a Roth IRA than in a taxable brokerage account. Get both straight before analysing
anything.

Jurisdiction matters — account types and tax rules below are primarily US, with UK equivalents
noted. Confirm current limits and rules against the tax authority; they change annually.

## Asset classes

The building blocks, by role in a portfolio:

| Class | Role | Long-run character |
|---|---|---|
| **Equities (stocks)** | Growth | Highest expected return, highest volatility and drawdown. The engine of a long-horizon portfolio |
| **Fixed income (bonds)** | Stability, income | Lower return, lower volatility; cushions equity drawdowns — usually, not always (2022 fell together) |
| **Cash / money market** | Liquidity, safety | Near-zero real return; preserves capital and optionality |
| **Real assets (real estate, commodities, TIPS)** | Inflation hedge, diversification | Varies; REITs are equity-like, commodities are volatile and produce no cash flow |
| **Alternatives** | Diversification, return | Private equity, hedge funds, crypto — high cost, low transparency, often illiquid; scrutinise hard |

Within equities, the axes that matter for diversification and factor exposure: **geography**
(domestic / international developed / emerging), **size** (large / mid / small cap), **style**
(value / growth), and **sector**. A "total market" fund spans size and style domestically; global
diversification needs international exposure too.

## Fund structures

Most independent investors should access asset classes through **pooled funds**, not individual
securities — instant diversification, low cost, no single-company research burden.

- **Index fund** — holds a market index mechanically. Low cost, broad, tax-efficient, no manager
  risk. The default building block.
- **Actively managed fund** — a manager picks holdings aiming to beat a benchmark. Higher cost;
  most underperform their index after fees over the long run (see below).
- **Target-date fund (TDF)** — a single fund holding a diversified mix that automatically shifts
  from equities toward bonds as a target retirement year approaches. A complete, hands-off
  portfolio in one ticker; excellent default for a retirement account. Check the **glide path** and
  the underlying expense ratio.
- **Balanced / allocation fund** — a fixed stock/bond mix (e.g. 60/40) in one fund.

## Index vs active

The evidence here is unusually strong and worth stating plainly, because it drives most sound
independent-investor design: **the large majority of active funds underperform their benchmark
index over 10–15 year periods, after fees**, and the persistence of the winners is weak — past
outperformance barely predicts future. The mechanism is simple arithmetic (the average active
dollar earns the market return *before* costs, so *after* costs it must trail) plus the drag of
higher fees and taxes.

This does not make active investing irrational in every niche (some inefficient or illiquid corners
have a better case), but it does mean the **burden of proof is on active**: an active fund has to
justify its fee with a durable, explainable edge, and "it beat the market last five years" is not
that. Default to low-cost index exposure and make complexity argue for itself.

## ETFs vs mutual funds

Often holding identical index strategies, but with mechanical differences:

| | ETF | Mutual fund |
|---|---|---|
| Trading | Intraday on an exchange, like a stock | Once daily at NAV |
| Minimums | One share (or fractional) | Often a dollar minimum |
| Tax efficiency (taxable accounts) | Usually higher — in-kind creation/redemption limits capital-gains distributions | Can distribute capital gains you didn't trigger |
| Cost | Very low for index ETFs | Very low for index funds; can match |
| Automatic investing | Sometimes clunkier | Easy recurring purchases |

For a **taxable** account the ETF's structural tax efficiency is a genuine edge. In a **tax-advantaged**
account it's irrelevant, and a mutual fund's easy automatic investing can win. Neither is
universally better — it depends on the account.

Watch **bid-ask spreads and premiums/discounts** on thinly traded ETFs, and never market-order an
illiquid ETF at the open or close; use limit orders.

## Bonds and fixed income

Return comes from **yield**, and the price moves inversely to interest rates. Two primary risks:

- **Interest-rate risk** — measured by **duration** (roughly, % price change per 1% rate move). A
  20-year Treasury fund can fall sharply when rates rise, as 2022 showed; a short-duration fund
  barely moves. Match duration to horizon.
- **Credit risk** — the chance the issuer defaults. Treasuries (near-zero credit risk) → investment-grade
  corporates → high-yield ("junk"). Higher yield is compensation for higher default risk, and
  high-yield behaves more like equity in a crisis.

Vehicles: individual bonds (hold to maturity, known cash flows, no expense ratio but harder to
diversify), bond funds/ETFs (diversified, liquid, but no fixed maturity), Treasury bills and
**TIPS** (inflation-protected principal), and CDs. For most independent investors a broad
investment-grade bond fund plus, optionally, short Treasuries or TIPS covers the need simply.

## Cash and equivalents

Not a failure to invest — a position. **Money market funds**, high-yield savings, T-bills, and CDs
hold the emergency fund and near-term spending, and preserve optionality. In higher-rate
environments cash yields are real and worth capturing (e.g. a T-bill ladder or a money market
fund) rather than leaving cash idle at 0% in a brokerage sweep. The trade-off is long-run: cash
loses to inflation over decades, so it's for liquidity and safety, not growth.

## Alternatives

Private equity, private credit, hedge funds, venture, commodities, collectibles, and crypto.
Approach with skepticism proportional to the sales pitch:

- **Costs are high and often opaque** (the classic "2 and 20"), and fees this size are a severe
  headwind.
- **Illiquidity** can lock capital for years, and reported "low volatility" is often just
  infrequent, smoothed marking — not genuinely lower risk.
- **Access and diligence** are hard for independent investors; the best funds are often closed.
- **Crypto** is highly volatile, largely uncorrelated to fundamentals, and to be sized as
  speculation an investor can afford to lose entirely — not as a core holding.

The honest default: a well-diversified low-cost portfolio of public stocks and bonds captures most
of the risk premia available, and most alternatives add cost and complexity faster than
diversification benefit. Where an alternative genuinely fits, size it small and understand exactly
what you're paying and what can go wrong.

## Account types and the tax wrapper

Where you hold an asset changes its after-tax return more than most investment choices do. US
types (UK equivalents noted):

| Account | Tax treatment | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Taxable brokerage** | Dividends and realised gains taxed yearly; long-term gains favoured | Flexibility, no contribution limit, goals before retirement |
| **Traditional 401(k)/IRA** | Contributions pre-tax, growth tax-deferred, withdrawals taxed as income | Deferring tax to a lower-bracket retirement |
| **Roth 401(k)/IRA** | Contributions after-tax, growth and qualified withdrawals **tax-free** | Long horizons, expected higher future bracket |
| **HSA** | Triple tax-advantaged (deductible in, tax-free growth, tax-free for medical) | Often the single best US account if eligible |
| **529** | Tax-free growth for education | Education goals |
| **UK: ISA** | Tax-free growth and withdrawals, annual allowance | The default UK tax-free wrapper |
| **UK: SIPP / pension** | Tax relief in, taxed on withdrawal | Retirement |

General priority logic (not personalised advice — the framework): capture any **employer match**
first (it's free return), then weigh HSA and tax-advantaged space against a taxable account by
comparing current vs expected future tax rates. The right order depends on the individual's bracket
and goals, which is where a fiduciary earns their fee.

## Asset location

Given the same holdings across a taxable and a tax-advantaged account, *which* asset sits *where*
affects after-tax return — "asset location," distinct from asset allocation:

- **Tax-inefficient assets** (taxable bonds, REITs, high-turnover funds — they throw off ordinary
  income) are better held in **tax-advantaged** accounts.
- **Tax-efficient assets** (broad equity index funds/ETFs — low distributions, gains deferred until
  sale, favourable long-term rates) can sit in **taxable** accounts.

This is a real, controllable edge for investors holding both account types, and a good example of
process-over-prediction: it adds after-tax return without forecasting anything. See
[costs-taxes-behavior.md](costs-taxes-behavior.md).
