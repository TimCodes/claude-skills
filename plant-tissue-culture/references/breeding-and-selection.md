## Two different games

People say "breeding unique characteristics" to mean two opposite operations, and confusing them
wastes enormous effort. Establish which one is in play before advising:

**Finding and fixing novelty** — generate variation (somaclonal, induced mutation, polyploidy,
or sexual crossing), screen for something desirable, then stabilize it. Here variation is the
product, and the risky, unstable techniques are the useful ones.

**Faithfully multiplying an existing trait** — someone has a variegated plant or a good seedling
and wants 500 identical ones. Here variation is the enemy, and every technique in the first
category is a threat. Nodal propagation, low subculture ceilings, and low PGRs.

Most commercial pain comes from running the second job with the first job's tools — leaf
explants and high cytokinin on a chimeral variegate, producing a flask of plants that don't
match the mother.

## Variegation and chimeras

This is the highest-value and most misunderstood area in ornamental tissue culture, and it's
where most money is lost.

### Know which kind of variegation you have

| Type | Mechanism | Behavior in culture |
|---|---|---|
| **Chimeral** (periclinal) | Genetically distinct cell layers (L1/L2/L3) — typically a mutant, plastid-defective layer over a normal one | **Unstable.** Preserved only through pre-existing meristems. Adventitious regeneration reverts it to solid green or solid albino |
| **Genetic / nuclear** | A stable nuclear gene affecting pigment or plastid development, present in every cell | Stable. Propagates faithfully by any method, and comes true from seed if homozygous |
| **Transposon-mediated** | A mobile element inserting and excising in a pigment gene | Sectoral and irregular; pattern varies plant to plant. Heritable but not reproducible in detail |
| **Viral** | Virus infection producing mosaic | Transmissible. Not a cultivar; usually a liability, and knowingly selling virused stock is a serious problem |
| **Environmental / physiological** | Nutrient deficiency, light, cold | Not heritable at all. Disappears when conditions are corrected |

Most prized aroid, *Monstera*, and *Philodendron* variegation is **chimeral**, which is exactly
why those plants stayed expensive: they resist the cheap mass-propagation route.

Diagnostics: chimeral variegation shows sharp boundaries following cell lineages, produces
sectored leaves and whole reverted branches, and is inconsistent between offsets. Genetic
variegation is even, consistent across the plant, and consistent in progeny.

### Propagating a chimera through culture

The instability is structural. A periclinal chimera holds its layers in place only because the
shoot apical meristem keeps them organized. Any regeneration that builds a new meristem from a
single cell — which is what adventitious regeneration does — produces a plant of whatever layer
that cell came from, so you get solid green or non-viable albino.

Practical consequences:

- **Use nodal segments and axillary buds only.** No leaf, internode, or callus explants, ever.
- **Keep cytokinin low.** High cytokinin, and TDZ especially, promotes adventitious shoots even
  from nodal explants — the mechanism you're trying to avoid, arriving through the back door.
- **Screen every subculture visually** and cull reverted and over-white shoots. Selection is
  continuous, not one-time.
- **Expect ongoing reversion loss** and price it in. A 20–40% off-type rate per cycle is normal
  for a good chimera, and it is a permanent operating cost, not a fixable defect.
- **Balance is the product.** Fully albino sectors have no chloroplasts and can't sustain
  themselves; over-white plants die in Stage IV or grow so slowly they're unsellable. Select the
  middle, not the extreme.
- **Keep a conservative mother block** ex vitro. When the in vitro line drifts — and it will —
  re-initiate from the mother rather than trying to select the line back into shape.

Be direct with users about this: a chimeral variegate cannot be mass-produced the way a stable
cultivar can. Anyone promising 10,000 uniform units from a chimera is either using a stable
mutant or about to disappoint their customers.

### Getting a stable variegate

The durable path is to find or create *genetic* variegation — a nuclear mutation that every cell
carries. Routes: screen large seedling populations from a variegated parent, screen somaclonal
variants (below), or induce mutations. This is slow and low-yield, and it produces something
that can actually be scaled and protected, which is where the real commercial value sits.

## Somaclonal variation as a tool

Somaclonal variation is the genetic and epigenetic change that accumulates in culture. Normally
a defect; deliberately, it's a cheap source of novelty requiring no mutagens.

What increases it: callus and adventitious regeneration (rather than axillary), 2,4-D, high
cytokinin, long culture duration, high subculture number, and stress (osmotic, temperature,
selective agents).

To use it deliberately: run explants through a callus phase with 2,4-D, regenerate a large
population, and screen for phenotype. Then — and this is the step people skip — **verify
stability** by growing selections out ex vitro for at least one full growth cycle and
re-propagating. Many somaclonal "variants" are epigenetic and revert once out of culture, so a
promising flask means nothing until it survives that test.

Yield is low: thousands of regenerants for a handful of interesting phenotypes, most of them
worse than the parent. It's cheap per unit, so it works when you can screen at scale.

## Induced mutagenesis

When somaclonal rates are too low, mutate deliberately. In vitro material is ideal — you can
treat thousands of uniform propagules and regenerate immediately.

**Chemical — EMS (ethyl methanesulfonate)**, typically 0.1–1% for 1–4 h on nodes, seeds, or
callus, followed by thorough washing. Produces point mutations at high density. **EMS is a
potent mutagen and a human carcinogen** — use a fume hood, double gloves, dedicated glassware,
and inactivate all waste (sodium thiosulfate or strong NaOH) before disposal.

**Physical — gamma or X-ray irradiation**, typically 10–50 Gy for in vitro material. Produces
deletions and chromosome rearrangements. Requires an irradiation facility; many agricultural
universities and national nuclear agencies provide this service to breeders.

**Dose calibration is mandatory.** Run a kill curve first and target the **LD₅₀** — the dose
killing about half the treated material. Below it you get too few mutations to be worth
screening; above it, survivors are so damaged that nothing is usable. Skipping the kill curve is
the standard way these programs fail.

**Chimera dissolution:** a mutagenized shoot is a mosaic, because the mutation happened in one
cell of a multicellular meristem. Subculture through **at least 3–4 vegetative generations**
(M1V1 → M1V4), selecting the sector of interest each time, before evaluating. Sectors that look
solid at M1V1 routinely fall apart later, and evaluating too early wastes the whole screen.

## Polyploid induction

Doubling chromosome number is one of the most reliable routes to a distinctly different plant:
typically thicker and darker leaves, larger flowers, shorter internodes, slower growth, and
often improved substance and vase life. It also restores fertility in sterile interspecific
hybrids, which unlocks crosses that were previously dead ends.

**Antimitotic agents** disrupt spindle formation so chromosomes duplicate without the cell
dividing.

| Agent | In vitro range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Colchicine** | 0.01–0.2% (100–2000 mg/L), 24–48 h | The classic. Highly toxic to humans, poor affinity for plant tubulin, so it needs high doses |
| **Oryzalin** | 5–50 µM, 24–72 h | A dinitroaniline herbicide with much higher plant tubulin affinity. Effective at far lower doses, less toxic to the operator. **Generally the better choice** |
| **Trifluralin** | 5–50 µM | Similar to oryzalin |
| **APM** (amiprophos-methyl) | 1–30 µM | Similar; sometimes better on monocots |

Method: treat actively dividing tissue — meristems, nodal explants, or germinating seed — either
by soaking or by incorporating the agent into the medium. Actively dividing is essential; the
agents only act on cells in mitosis, so dormant material gives nothing. Rinse thoroughly, then
regenerate.

Expect: high mortality, and among survivors a mix of true polyploids, mixoploids (mosaics of
2n and 4n cells), and unchanged diploids. **Mixoploids are the common outcome** and they're
unstable — subculture them through several generations, screening at each pass, until the sector
is uniform. Selling a mixoploid as a tetraploid produces a cultivar that changes on the
customer's windowsill.

Handling: colchicine and the dinitroanilines are hazardous — gloves, hood, dedicated glassware,
labelled waste stream, no drain disposal. Say so whenever you give one of these protocols.

**Triploids** (3n), from a 4n × 2n cross, are typically sterile and vigorous — the route to
seedless fruit and non-invasive sterile ornamentals, which is increasingly a regulatory selling
point for species otherwise banned as invasive.

## Ploidy verification

Phenotype is suggestive, never sufficient — many treated plants merely look chunky from stress.

- **Flow cytometry** — the standard. Chop fresh leaf tissue in a nuclei-isolation buffer, stain
  with DAPI or propidium iodide, and read against a known-ploidy internal standard run in the
  same tube. Fast, quantitative, and detects mixoploidy as a mixed peak. Many universities and
  commercial labs run samples for a modest fee, which is usually better than buying a cytometer.
- **Guard cell chloroplast count** — count chloroplasts in stomatal guard cells on a leaf
  epidermal peel. Polyploids have measurably more (often roughly double). Cheap, needs only a
  light microscope, good for screening large populations before confirming a shortlist by
  cytometry.
- **Stomatal size and density** — polyploids have larger, less dense stomata. A quick screen,
  but environmentally sensitive, so use it to rank rather than to conclude.
- **Chromosome counting** — root tip squash, pretreated and stained (aceto-orcein or Feulgen).
  Definitive and laborious. Reserve it for publication or for species without a ploidy standard.

Verify **twice, months apart**, for anything mixoploid-suspect. Sectors resolve over time and an
early reading can be flatly wrong.

## Compact and dwarf habit

Compactness sells — it cuts shipping volume, bench space, and grower effort. Several routes,
with very different durability:

- **Genetic dwarfs** from mutagenesis or somaclonal screening — permanent, and the real prize.
  Screen regenerant populations for short internodes that persist ex vitro through a full cycle.
- **Polyploidy** — often gives shorter internodes as a side effect.
- **Chemical growth retardants** (paclobutrazol, ancymidol, daminozide) — these produce compact
  plants that are **not** genetically compact. The effect wears off, and a customer who buys a
  chemically-dwarfed plant gets a full-size plant next season. Legitimate as a production
  finishing treatment for a plant sold as-is; dishonest if presented as a cultivar trait.
- **Rootstock and grafting** — outside tissue culture, but often the right answer for woody
  plants.

When a user wants a compact cultivar, ask whether they want a genotype or a look. The answer
picks the route and determines whether they have something they can name and protect.

## Conventional breeding supported by culture

Tissue culture is a powerful support system for ordinary sexual breeding:

- **Embryo rescue** — in wide or interspecific crosses the embryo often aborts because the
  endosperm fails. Excise it 2–4 weeks post-pollination and culture it on a rich medium
  (MS with reduced sucrose, sometimes with coconut water). This is how many otherwise impossible
  hybrids exist at all.
- **Ovule and ovary culture** — for even earlier abortion, or where the embryo is too small to
  dissect.
- **Anther and microspore culture** — regenerates haploids from pollen; chromosome doubling then
  gives a fully homozygous **doubled haploid** in one generation instead of six-plus of selfing.
  Transformative for line development, though strongly genotype-dependent and simply
  non-functional in many species.
- **Ovule/protoplast fusion** — somatic hybridization across barriers sexual crossing can't
  bridge. Technically demanding and rarely worth it outside a research program.
- **Rapid multiplication of a single elite seedling** — the everyday case. A breeder gets one
  outstanding F1; culture turns that single plant into a marketable population in 18 months
  instead of a decade.
- **Rescue of a dying accession** — sometimes the last individual of a genotype is failing, and
  culture is the only way to save it. Take nodes early rather than heroically late.

## Selection, stabilization, and trialling

A new phenotype in a flask is a candidate, not a cultivar. Before it's worth investing in:

1. **Stabilize** — carry it through several vegetative generations, selecting the target sector
   each time, until it's uniform.
2. **Grow it out ex vitro** for at least one full growth cycle. Culture-induced phenotypes —
   epigenetic effects, PGR carryover, hyperhydricity artefacts — routinely evaporate here. This
   step disqualifies most candidates and saves the most money.
3. **Re-propagate from the stabilized plant** and confirm the trait transmits.
4. **Verify ploidy and, where relevant, virus status.**
5. **Trial under real grower conditions** — multiple sites and seasons if the market warrants.
   Vigor, disease resistance, shipping tolerance, and shelf life decide commercial success far
   more often than novelty does.
6. **Document distinctness, uniformity, and stability (DUS)** with dated photographs and
   measurements against the closest comparison cultivar. This is both good record-keeping and
   the evidentiary basis for any later protection application.

Keep a **candidate log** — every selection with its origin, date, treatment, parent line,
subculture number, and observations. Programs generating hundreds of candidates lose track
within a year without one, and an unattributable plant can't be protected or honestly sold.

## Protecting and naming a cultivar

Once something is real, the protection routes differ by jurisdiction — see
[sales-and-compliance.md](sales-and-compliance.md) for the commercial and legal side, including
the critical constraint that **public disclosure or sale can start a clock that forfeits
protection rights.** Talk to the user about protection *before* they post it on Instagram, not
after; this is one of the most common and most expensive unforced errors in ornamental breeding.
