## The five stages

Murashige's staging is still the organizing frame, and each stage has its own dominant failure
mode. Naming the stage narrows the diagnosis immediately.

| Stage | What happens | Dominant failure |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Mother plant preparation | Skipped entirely, which shows up as Stage I contamination |
| I | Establishment / initiation | Contamination, phenolic browning, explant death |
| II | Multiplication | Hyperhydricity, low multiplication rate, somaclonal drift |
| III | Rooting and elongation | No roots, or callus-mediated roots that don't connect vascularly |
| IV | Acclimatization / deflasking | Desiccation collapse in the first 72 hours |

## Stage 0 — mother plant preparation

The most commonly skipped stage and the highest-leverage one. Everything living on or in the
donor plant is what you'll fight for the next six months.

For 2–4 weeks before taking explants:

- Move the mother plant under cover, out of rain and splash. Water at the soil only — overhead
  watering drives soil microbes onto the tissue you're about to harvest.
- Lower humidity and improve airflow to suppress surface fungi.
- Apply a systemic fungicide and consider a bactericide drench, on the label's schedule.
- Push clean, vigorous new growth with balanced fertility. Actively growing juvenile tissue
  establishes far better than mature or stressed tissue.
- If the plant may carry virus, index it before investing. Tissue culture multiplies viruses as
  faithfully as it multiplies plants, and a virused mother stock poisons an entire program. For
  cleanup, see meristem culture and thermotherapy below.

Harvest explants in the morning from actively elongating shoots, and process them within the
hour.

**Meristem culture for virus elimination:** excise the apical dome plus 1–2 leaf primordia, at
0.2–0.5 mm — small enough to be ahead of the vascular tissue the virus moves through. Combine
with thermotherapy (35–38 °C for 2–6 weeks before excision) and/or chemotherapy (ribavirin in
the medium) for difficult viruses. Survival is low and growth is slow; expect to sacrifice many
explants. Always re-index the resulting line before declaring it clean — assuming success here
is how virused stock gets sold.

## Stage I — establishment

Goal: a sterile, living, growing explant. Contamination and browning are the two enemies and
they pull in opposite directions — harsher sterilization means less contamination and more dead
tissue — so Stage I is fundamentally a titration between them.

Run **more explants than you need**, in individual vessels rather than shared ones. One
contaminant in a shared vessel takes the whole vessel; the marginal cost of separate tubes is
trivial against the cost of losing a genotype.

Expect losses. For a well-characterized species, 60–80% clean establishment is good; for a
recalcitrant woody species from field material, 10–20% is normal and not a sign you did
anything wrong.

Hold new cultures in **quarantine** — physically separate from the main culture room — for at
least 3–4 weeks. Latent bacterial endophytes routinely take 2–6 weeks to become visible, and
sometimes only surface after the first subculture. Never move an explant into the main room on
its first clean-looking week.

## Explant choice

The explant determines the genetic risk more than any other choice, which matters enormously
for anything sold as a named cultivar or for chimeral variegation.

| Explant | Regeneration path | Genetic fidelity |
|---|---|---|
| **Nodal segment / axillary bud** | Pre-existing meristem | Highest. Preserves chimeras. The default for true-to-type work |
| **Shoot tip / apical meristem** | Pre-existing meristem | Highest; also the virus-elimination route |
| **Leaf, internode, root, petiole** | Adventitious — new meristems form de novo | Lower. **Destroys chimeral variegation.** Higher somaclonal variation |
| **Callus** | Indirect, via unorganized tissue | Lowest. Use only when variation is the goal, or when nothing else works |
| **Somatic embryo** | Bipolar embryo from somatic cell | Variable; excellent for scale-up and synthetic seed, but the callus phase carries variation risk |
| **Seed / embryo** | Zygotic | Not clonal at all — segregating progeny. This is a breeding tool, not a propagation tool |

The single most important line in this table: **adventitious regeneration destroys periclinal
chimeras.** If the trait being propagated is chimeral variegation, leaf explants will give you
a flask of solid-green or solid-white plants. Use nodes.

Juvenile tissue outperforms mature tissue, often dramatically, in woody species. If a mature
tree won't establish, force juvenile growth first — hedge it hard, use epicormic shoots or root
suckers, or graft scions onto seedling rootstock and take the resulting flush.

## Surface sterilization

The general sequence, adjusted per species:

1. **Wash** under running tap water 10–30 min, with a drop of detergent. This physically removes
   most of the load and is routinely undervalued.
2. **Trim** to slightly larger than the final explant. Cut surfaces are entry points, so
   minimize them and re-trim after sterilization.
3. **70% ethanol, 30–60 seconds.** A wetting/dewaxing step, not the main kill. Longer is
   phytotoxic — ethanol penetrates fast.
4. **Sodium hypochlorite**, the main kill: **0.5–1.5% available chlorine for 10–20 minutes**,
   with 2–3 drops/L of Tween 20 as a surfactant. Household bleach is typically 5–8% NaOCl, so a
   10–20% v/v dilution lands in range — but bleach loses strength on the shelf, so an old bottle
   silently under-sterilizes. Agitate throughout.
5. **Rinse** 3–4 times in sterile distilled water, 5 min each, inside the hood.
6. **Re-trim** the bleach-damaged cut ends with a sterile blade before plating.

Adjustments: soft herbaceous tissue → shorter and weaker (0.5%, 5–10 min). Woody, hairy, or
field-collected material → longer and stronger, possibly two rounds, sometimes with a brief
fungicide soak first. Seeds and hard structures tolerate much more.

**Mercuric chloride** (0.1%, 3–8 min) is used in some published protocols for recalcitrant
material. It works, and it is acutely toxic and an environmental hazard with disposal
obligations. Treat it as a last resort after NaOCl variants and a proper Stage 0 have failed,
and if using it, say what the disposal requirement is.

If contamination persists after a proper sterilization series, the organism is almost certainly
**endophytic** — living inside the tissue, where surface sterilants cannot reach. No amount of
extra bleach fixes this. Go back to Stage 0, try meristem-sized explants, or change the source
plant.

## Stage II — multiplication

Subculture every 4–6 weeks onto fresh medium; the interval is set by when the medium exhausts
and shoots crowd, which you learn by watching rather than by calendar.

**Multiplication rate** is the economic engine. A rate of 3× per 5-week cycle is ~10 cycles from
one explant to 59,000 plants; a rate of 2× needs ~16 cycles for the same. Track it per genotype
— see [monitoring-and-troubleshooting.md](monitoring-and-troubleshooting.md).

But rate trades against quality. Pushing cytokinin to raise the rate buys hyperhydricity,
rooting inhibition, and somaclonal variation. The right target is the highest rate that still
yields shoots that root well and stay true-to-type — usually well below the maximum achievable.

Cap the **subculture number**. Somaclonal variation accumulates with cycles, so commercial labs
typically discard a line and restart from cryopreserved or in vivo mother stock after 8–12
subcultures. Track the count on every vessel label from the beginning; reconstructing it later
is impossible.

## Stage III — rooting

Reduce salts (½MS), reduce sucrose (15–20 g/L), remove cytokinin, add auxin (IBA 0.5–1 mg/L).

Prefer an **auxin pulse** — 24–72 h on high auxin, or a brief concentrated dip, then PGR-free
medium. Continuous auxin produces a callus plug at the shoot base, and roots that arise from
callus often lack vascular continuity with the shoot: they look like success in the flask and
fail in Stage IV, because water can't actually move through the junction. If plants with good
roots collapse on deflasking, suspect exactly this.

Residual cytokinin carried over from Stage II is the most common cause of rooting failure. Give
a PGR-free elongation pass, or add 1–2 g/L activated charcoal, before blaming the auxin.

Many easy species root better and cheaper **ex vitro** — skip Stage III, dip microcuttings in
rooting hormone, and root them directly in the acclimatization environment. This combines two
stages, cuts cost substantially, and produces better-adapted roots. Worth testing for anything
that roots readily from conventional cuttings.

## Stage IV — acclimatization

Where most losses happen in small labs, and it is entirely a water-relations problem. Plants
grown in ~100% humidity on sugar develop a thin, poorly-formed cuticle, non-functional stomata
that don't close on demand, and roots adapted to agar. Moved to ambient air, they desiccate in
hours.

The protocol:

1. **Open the vessel gradually** — loosen closures for 2–5 days in the culture room before
   removing plants. A cheap and effective hardening step.
2. **Wash all agar off the roots** in lukewarm water. Residual agar is a sugar-rich substrate
   that grows mold around the root collar within days.
3. **Pot into a sterile, free-draining, low-nutrient mix** — fine bark, perlite, peat/coir,
   vermiculite. Sterile matters; these plants have no microbiome and no defenses yet.
4. **Hold near 100% humidity for the first 5–7 days** — humidity dome, fog, or mist. Then step
   humidity *down* over 2–3 weeks. The step-down is the actual hardening; leaving plants at 100%
   indefinitely just delays the failure.
5. **Reduce light initially** (50–100 µmol/m²/s), raising it as the plants harden. Full light on
   day one bleaches them.
6. **Begin dilute fertigation at ~week 2**, once new growth appears. New growth, not survival,
   is the signal that roots are functioning.
7. **A preventative fungicide drench** is cheap insurance in the first two weeks.

Expect 70–95% survival with good technique. If it's much lower, look upstream at Stage III root
quality before changing anything in Stage IV.

## Environment

Standard culture room conditions:

- **Temperature** 24–26 °C, stable. Cool-climate and alpine species may want 18–22 °C
- **Photoperiod** 16 h light / 8 h dark
- **Light** 30–60 µmol/m²/s PPFD at shelf level for Stage I–II; 60–100 for Stage III to promote
  photoautotrophy. Cool white LED or fluorescent
- **Gas exchange** — vessel closure is a top-three and consistently underrated variable. Sealed
  vessels accumulate ethylene and hold humidity at saturation, which is the leading environmental
  cause of hyperhydricity. Vented closures or gas-permeable membranes fix a surprising number of
  chronic quality problems

## Aseptic technique

- Laminar flow hood, HEPA-filtered, run 15+ min before use. Sterilize the deck with 70% ethanol
  and work at least 15 cm inside the front edge.
- Never let hands, sleeves, or vessels pass *over* open medium — the airflow is horizontal and
  anything upstream sheds into the vessel.
- Flame-sterilize instruments between every explant, or use a glass bead sterilizer. Cool them
  before touching tissue; a hot scalpel cooks the cut surface.
- Keep vessels open for as short a time as possible, angled rather than upright.
- Work clean-to-dirty: new establishments before subcultures, and never open a suspect vessel in
  the hood at all — remove it from the room sealed.
- If a contamination spike appears, check the HEPA filter, the hood's airflow, and the autoclave's
  actual achieved temperature with an indicator strip before blaming technique. Autoclaves fail
  quietly and gradually.

## Designing a protocol for a new species

1. **Search the literature first**, including for congeners. A related species' protocol is a far
   better starting point than first principles. See [research-methods.md](research-methods.md).
2. **Classify the plant**: herbaceous vs. woody, monocot vs. dicot, known browning tendency, known
   virus issues. This picks the base medium.
3. **Start conservative on sterilization** and titrate up. Losing explants to contamination is
   recoverable; killing every explant with over-sterilization on a rare genotype is not.
4. **Run a small factorial on the cytokinin** in Stage II — typically 4 levels (0, 0.5, 1, 2
   mg/L BAP) × 10 replicates. This one experiment answers the most important open question.
5. **Change one variable per iteration** thereafter, and write down what you did and what
   happened, including failures. Failures carry the most information and are the first thing
   people neglect to record.
