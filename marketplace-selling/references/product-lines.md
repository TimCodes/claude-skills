## Plant tissue culture plantlets

**Best channel:** Etsy, and Facebook Marketplace for local. **Not Amazon** — live plants are
largely prohibited or gated there.

**What sells:** rare and variegated houseplants (aroids especially), plantlets in flask or
deflasked plugs, and tissue-culture starter cultures for other growers. The collector market
pays premiums for scarcity — and that premium falls as the plant becomes common, which the
`plant-tissue-culture` skill covers in depth.

**Shipping is the crux.** Live plants have a **live-arrival guarantee** expectation, need
**weather holds** (heat and cold both kill), ship best early in the week to avoid weekend
transit, and want fast service (2-day where the margin allows). Selling **in flask** pushes the
acclimatisation risk onto the buyer and ships robustly, but generates support questions —
include care instructions in every order.

**Compliance — this is the line with the most rules:**
- **Interstate shipment** of nursery stock is regulated; some states (California, Florida,
  Arizona, Oregon, Hawaii among the strictest) require a phytosanitary certificate, a nursery
  licence, or prohibit certain genera outright. A plant legal at origin can be prohibited at
  destination.
- Many US states require a **nursery/plant dealer licence** to sell plants at all, including
  online.
- **International** shipping needs phytosanitary certificates and often import permits; CITES
  covers all orchids and cacti (with some sterile-flask exemptions).
- Selling **patented or PBR-protected cultivars** without a licence is infringement, and tissue
  culture is the most provable way to do it.

For the detail — licensing, phyto rules, per-state constraints, CITES, and pricing against a
falling scarcity premium — hand off to the `plant-tissue-culture` skill's
[sales-and-compliance reference](../../plant-tissue-culture/references/sales-and-compliance.md).
This skill's job is the listing, the channel choice, and the shipping economics.

## 3D-printed goods

**Best channel:** Etsy for decorative/custom/niche; Amazon or FB Shops for a repeatable SKU
with demand; Facebook Marketplace for bulky prints.

**What sells:** custom and personalised items, functional parts not otherwise available,
original designs, and niche décor. Generic models (articulated dragons, common Thingiverse
prints) are a race to the bottom — everyone prints them, and the margin is near zero.

**Margin structure:** the trap is that **labour, not filament, is the main cost** —
post-processing (support removal, sanding, finishing) usually exceeds material and machine
cost combined, and it's the most-omitted line. Price the labour, not the plastic.

**Compliance:**
- **Model licensing.** You may only sell prints the licence permits — **CC-BY-NC forbids
  commercial use entirely.** Downloading a model is not a licence to sell it.
- **Trademarked/copyrighted characters** — fan art infringes regardless of who modelled it.
- **Food-contact** items are sellable done right (sealed/coated, stainless nozzle), but
  "food-safe filament" alone does not make a food-safe part.
- **No safety-critical or load-bearing** parts where failure hurts someone.

For print costing, the licence table, and food-contact detail, hand off to the `3d-printing`
skill's [selling reference](../../3d-printing/references/selling-and-licensing.md). This skill
covers merchandising and moving the finished goods.

## Terrariums

**Best channel:** Etsy for shippable sizes; **Facebook Marketplace for large ones** — local
hand-off removes the biggest problem entirely.

**What sells:** closed ecosystem jars, moss terrariums, and planted glass as décor and as gifts
(strong seasonal spikes). Custom and themed builds command the best margins.

**Shipping is brutal and defines the economics.** A terrarium is **heavy glass around live
plants and often loose substrate** — it's fragile, dimensionally weight-costly, and the plants
have the same weather constraints as plantlets. Real considerations:
- Ship in a **sealed, cushioned, double-boxed** package; substrate must be secured so it
  doesn't shift and crush the planting.
- **Closed terrariums** tolerate transit better (self-contained humidity); open ones dry and
  spill.
- Dimensional weight and breakage push shipping cost high — often **the largest single cost**,
  and a leading return/refund cause. Build the real, breakage-adjusted shipping figure into
  every quote, and consider a build-size ceiling above which you only sell locally.
- A stated **live-arrival and breakage policy** up front prevents most disputes.

**Compliance:** the live-plant rules above apply to the plants inside (phyto, per-state,
licensing). Facebook has tripped sellers over "live" listings — keep claims factual. Don't ship
anything with pest risk across state lines.

## Small woodworking projects

**Best channel:** Etsy for shippable décor and custom pieces; Facebook Marketplace for larger
or heavier pieces; Amazon Handmade only for a genuinely repeatable line.

**What sells:** cutting boards, coasters, small shelves, boxes, engraved and personalised gifts,
and custom commissions. Personalisation is the margin — a laser-engraved name turns a commodity
board into a gift someone pays a premium for.

**Margin and shipping:** wood is **heavy and shippable-but-costly**; large pieces are
better sold locally. As with printing, **your labour and finishing time is the real cost**, and
hand-made wood competes badly on price against mass production — compete on custom, quality, and
finish instead.

**Compliance:**
- **Finish safety for food-contact** boards and utensils — use food-safe finishes (mineral oil,
  beeswax, food-safe hard-wax); say so in the listing, since buyers ask.
- **Exotic/imported woods** can fall under the Lacey Act (US) and CITES (e.g. rosewood,
  ebony) — provenance matters for those species; common domestic hardwoods are unproblematic.
- **Toys for young children** trigger safety rules (CPSIA/EN 71) — small parts and finish
  chemistry both apply.

## Resold overstock and liquidation stock

**Best channel:** **Amazon** (if the category is un-gated and you have acceptable invoices) and
**Facebook Marketplace** (for local, bulky, or fast liquidation). **Not Etsy** — reselling new
manufactured goods violates Etsy's handmade/vintage policy.

**This is a fundamentally different business from the maker lines** — margin comes from *buying
well*, not making well, and most of the risk is at purchase, not sale.

**The economics that kill resellers:**
- **Sell-through is everything.** A pallet's headline margin assumes every unit sells. In
  reality a fraction never does, and the good units must carry the dead ones. Model the lot at
  a *realistic* sell-through (often 60–80% for mixed liquidation), not 100%.
- **Landed cost per unit** includes the lot price, shipping the pallet, and any sorting/repair.
- **Condition grade drives price and returns.** Liquidation stock is often customer returns —
  graded from new to salvage. Misgrading (listing a scratched item as new) causes returns,
  negative feedback, and account defects.

**Compliance and account risk — the highest of any line:**
- **Authenticity.** Amazon can demand **invoices from an authorised supplier** to prove genuine
  goods and un-gate a brand; liquidation invoices often don't qualify, making genuine stock
  *unsellable* on Amazon. **Verify gating and invoice acceptability before buying.**
- **Counterfeits** in liquidation lots are a real hazard — reselling one, even unknowingly, can
  mean a permanent ban and legal exposure. Inspect, and avoid brands and sources you can't
  vouch for.
- **Condition accuracy** — describe used/return items honestly; "new" has a strict meaning.
- **Prohibited/recalled/expired goods** slip into lots (recalled electronics, expired
  consumables, hazmat) — screen them out.

Track every lot in [assets/sourcing-log-template.csv](../assets/sourcing-log-template.csv) so a
supplier's *realised* margin and sell-through become visible; a supplier that looked great on
one lucky lot often doesn't survive three.

## Portfolio view

These lines balance each other, which is the strength of running them together:

- **Maker lines** (plants, terrariums, print, wood) — higher margin, capped by the maker's time
  and skill; slow to scale; brand-building.
- **Resale** — lower margin, scales with capital not time; fills the channels and the cash flow
  between maker sales; higher account risk.
- **Seasonality** — terrariums and personalised wood/print gifts spike into the holidays; plants
  peak in spring; resale can be timed to demand. Staggering them smooths the year.

When advising, keep the whole portfolio in view: the right move is often to push the
labour-light, high-margin line this month and let a slow resale lot go cheap on Facebook to free
cash and space, rather than optimising each line in isolation.
